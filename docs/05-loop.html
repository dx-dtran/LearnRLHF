The PPO loop

All eight building blocks, snapped together into a rhythm.

1The rhythm: collect, then squeeze

PPO alternates two phases. A rollout phase where the policy writes responses and everything about them is measured and frozen into buffers. Then an optimize phase where those buffers are replayed for K = 4 epochs of minibatch gradient steps. Then the buffers are thrown away, because the policy that generated them no longer exists.

Why not just take one big gradient step per rollout? Because rollouts are expensive — generation dominates wall-clock time — and one step wastes the data. Why not fifty epochs? Because after each step the live policy drifts from the one that produced the buffers; the ratios drift from 1, more tokens hit the clip, and the surrogate stops being a trustworthy stand-in for the true objective. K = 4 is the negotiated truce, and the clip fraction in your logs is where you watch the truce hold.

2Four models, 24 GB

The memory bill is dominated not by weights but by the optimizer and activations — and only the trainable models pay those costs. That asymmetry is why four GPT-2s fit at all.

The design choices that make the budget work: the value head shares the policy backbone (one trainable transformer, not two), activations are gradient-checkpointed (recompute instead of store), and the frozen pair never builds a graph — every reference and RM forward runs under torch.no_grad().

3Reading the dashboard

PPO does not fail loudly. The loss goes down either way; generation quality is the thing being destroyed. You catch it in the metrics, and the two stories look like this:

The healthy pattern: reward climbs and flattens, KL drifts up slowly toward its budget (~6 nats over a 256-token response), entropy eases down. The pathology: reward keeps climbing while KL goes vertical and entropy collapses — the policy found a phrase or format the RM overpays for and is spamming it. The fix is never "train more": raise β, lower the learning rate, and check what the RM is actually rewarding.

Why the leash exists The reward model is only trustworthy near the distribution it was trained on — SFT-like text. The KL penalty is what keeps the policy inside that trust region. An exploding KL means you are now optimizing the RM's errors, not its judgment.

4Did it actually work? (Module 6)

The final judgment is embarrassingly manual, and that is deliberate. Generate 20 held-out prompts through base, SFT, and RLHF side by side. Then self-annotate SFT-vs-RLHF pairs blinded — shuffle which column is which before reading, because knowing which model produced a response reliably biases you toward it. Target: RLHF wins at least 55%. Not 90 — with GPT-2 small, a 124M-parameter model, "visibly better at following instructions" is the honest ceiling.

If RLHF loses the blind test, the ablation order is: lower β (the leash may be too tight to learn anything), more PPO iterations, then a longer-trained RM. And reread the dashboard first — a run that reward-hacked will lose the win-rate no matter what you ablate.
