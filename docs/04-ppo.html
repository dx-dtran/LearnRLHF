PPO building blocks

Eight small functions. Each one has a picture, and each picture has a place where the gradient is zero on purpose.

1The cast

From here on, four copies of GPT-2 are in play. Keep their roles straight — half of all PPO confusion is forgetting which model produced which number.

Policy πgenerates responses; the thing being trained trainable
Value head Vpredicts reward-to-go from each token; shares the policy backbone trainable
Reference πreffrozen copy of SFT; the KL anchor frozen
Reward modelscores the finished response once frozen

2Rollouts: the log-prob is one position to the left

During a rollout you must record, for every generated token, the log-probability the policy assigned it. The trap: the distribution that produced token t lives in the logits at position t − 1. Read the logits at the token's own position instead and you get the distribution for the next token — everything downstream still runs, and trains, wrong.

3The KL leash: k1 and k3

The reward model is a flawed judge, so the policy is kept on a leash: every token pays a penalty for drifting from the reference. The per-token quantity is an estimate of KL from a single sample, and there are two estimators you will implement:

The division of labor: k1 goes in the reward (its gradient with respect to log π is exactly 1 — the cleanest possible penalty), and k3 goes in the logs (a per-batch KL readout that never confuses you with a negative value). InstructGPT penalizes with k1; so do you.

4Reward shaping: one spike and a rent payment

The environment gives exactly one real reward, at the end — the RM score of the whole response. Every token before that only pays KL rent. This per-token reward vector is what GAE consumes next.

5GAE: spreading the spike backwards

A single terminal reward is a terrible learning signal for token 3 of 200. The value head fixes that: it predicts, at every token, how the response will eventually score. GAE then builds per-token advantages from the chain of prediction errors δt = rt + γVt+1 − Vt, mixed over horizons by λ.

Derivation boundary — problem 4.4 The recursion At = δt + γλ At+1 and why returns Rt = At + Vt are the value target belong on paper in notes/04-ppo-gae.md, along with the 3-step hand example this widget is secretly showing you.

6The clipped surrogate

This is the loss PPO is named for. Each token's objective is the probability ratio against the rollout-time policy, times the advantage — but clipped: once the ratio has moved past 1 ± ε in the direction the advantage was pushing, the objective goes flat and the gradient switches off. The token has moved enough this round.

Read the two cases off the picture: for a good token (A > 0), the model stops being rewarded for pushing its probability up past 1 + ε, but if the ratio somehow fell, the gradient to recover is fully alive. For a bad token, symmetric: probability can be pushed down only so far per round. That one-sided flatness is the entire "trust region" — no constraint solver, just a min.

Grad-check note — problem 4.5 Your test suite needs the edge case this plot makes obvious: construct a batch where every ratio sits in the shaded region and assert the policy gradient is exactly zero.

8Entropy: the anti-collapse term

Everything above pushes probability mass toward whatever scored well. Left alone, the per-token distributions sharpen until sampling is deterministic — and exploration dies. The entropy bonus pays the model a little for staying spread out.

9The bug: normalizing over padding

Advantages get normalized per batch — subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation — computed over valid response tokens only. Rollout buffers are padded rectangles, and whatever sits in the padding is garbage. Include it in the statistics and every real advantage in the batch is silently rescaled.

This is problem 4.8's unit test, and it is the single most common PPO implementation bug: pad half the batch with garbage, assert the statistics do not move.
